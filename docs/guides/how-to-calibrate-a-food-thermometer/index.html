How to calibrate a food thermometer (and log it)

Updated 2026-10-25 · 6 min read · Based on the FDA Food Code

Short answer: put the probe in a glass packed with crushed ice and a little cold water, wait until the reading settles, and check that it reads 32°F (0°C). Under the FDA Food Code a Fahrenheit food thermometer must be accurate to ±2°F (§4-203.11(B)). If it's further off, adjust it or replace it, and write the check on a calibration log.

Why calibration matters for every other log

Every number on your fridge, hot-holding and cooling logs comes from a thermometer. A probe that reads 3°F low turns a 43°F cold rail into a "40°F" entry, and a 132°F soup into a "135°F" one. Inspectors know this, which is why many check your probe in ice water during an inspection and ask how you verify it.

The accuracy limits

DeviceAccuracy requiredFDA Food Code
Food thermometer, °F scale±2°F§4-203.11(B)
Food thermometer, °C or dual scale±1°C§4-203.11(A)
Air/water thermometer (fridge, freezer, hot water), °F±3°F§4-203.12(B)
Air/water thermometer, °C or dual scale±1.5°C§4-203.12(A)

The Code also requires food thermometers to be "calibrated in accordance with manufacturer's specifications as necessary to ensure their accuracy" (§4-502.11(B)), and requires a food thermometer to be provided and readily accessible (§4-302.12). It also says thin foods need a thin probe (a thermocouple or similar with a small-diameter tip), so a thick dial stem isn't enough for burgers and fish fillets.

Method 1: the ice-point check (recommended)

  1. Fill a tall glass to the top with crushed ice (not cubes with big gaps).
  2. Add cold water to just below the top of the ice and stir. You want a slush, not ice floating in water.
  3. Wait about 3 minutes.
  4. Put the probe in the center, at least 2 inches deep, without touching the sides or bottom. Stir gently.
  5. Wait until the reading stops changing (about 30 seconds for a dial, a few seconds for most digitals).
  6. It should read 32°F (0°C).
  7. If it's off: on a dial thermometer, hold the calibration nut under the head with pliers or the sheath's built-in wrench and turn the dial to 32°F. On a digital with a reset or calibration button, follow the maker's instructions. If it can't be adjusted and is outside ±2°F, replace it.
  8. Write it on the log: date, thermometer ID, reading, how far off, what you did, initials.

Method 2: the boiling-point check

Bring a deep pot of water to a full, rolling boil and put the probe in the center, at least 2 inches deep, away from the bottom. Water boils at 212°F (100°C) at sea level, but the boiling point drops roughly 1°F for every 500 feet of elevation. In a city around 5,000 feet, water boils at about 202°F. If you don't know your elevation precisely, use the ice-point method instead. It's more reliable and it checks the thermometer near the cold-holding range, where most of your readings are.

How often to calibrate

The Code says "as necessary" and points to the manufacturer. A routine many kitchens and HACCP plans use:

  • Weekly for every probe in regular use (some kitchens do it daily at opening)
  • After a drop or any knock to the head of a dial thermometer
  • When a reading looks wrong, for example a fridge that "suddenly" reads 30°F
  • When it goes from very hot to very cold often, if the maker recommends it
  • When you put a new thermometer into service

Fridge and freezer thermometers

Hanging and built-in thermometers are held to ±3°F. Check them by comparing with a calibrated probe in a glass of water left in the unit for a while, or by putting the hanging thermometer in an ice slurry. If a built-in display is off, have it serviced, and keep a separate hanging thermometer in the warmest part of the unit in the meantime.

Keeping thermometers in service

  • Clean and sanitize probes between foods, especially between raw and ready-to-eat.
  • Give each one an ID (P1, P2…) so the log shows which thermometer was checked.
  • Keep spare batteries and a spare probe. A dead probe on inspection day is a problem you can avoid for a few dollars.
  • Don't use glass thermometers in food unless they're encased in a shatterproof coating (§4-201.12).

FAQ

How often should food thermometers be calibrated?

The FDA Food Code says as necessary, per the manufacturer's specifications. Weekly checks plus a check after any drop is a common routine; your HACCP plan or health department may require more.

What should a thermometer read in ice water?

32°F (0°C). A Fahrenheit food thermometer within 30–34°F meets the ±2°F accuracy rule, but adjust it to 32°F if you can.

Do I need to keep a calibration log?

The Food Code doesn't require one by name. HACCP plans include verification records, and many inspectors ask how you know your thermometers are accurate, so a log is the easy answer.
