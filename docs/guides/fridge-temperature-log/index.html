Fridge temperature log: what to record, how often, and a free sheet

Updated 2026-10-25 · 7 min read · Based on the FDA Food Code

A fridge temperature log is the simplest food safety record a kitchen keeps, and the one inspectors ask for most. Done well, it takes about a minute a day. Done badly (blank days, identical numbers, no corrective actions), it can count against you.

The rule: 41°F or below

The FDA Food Code requires refrigerated time/temperature control for safety (TCS) food to be held at 41°F (5°C) or below (§3-501.16(A)(2)). TCS food includes meat, poultry, seafood, dairy, cooked rice and beans, cut melons, cut leafy greens, cut tomatoes and sprouts, among others. Most US states base their food code on a version of the FDA Food Code, though a few use older editions or their own numbers.

For freezers, the Code says food must stay frozen (§3-501.11). The usual target is 0°F (−18°C) or below. That's an industry best practice, not a number written into the Code.

Set fridges to about 36–38°F. That gives you a buffer for busy periods when doors are opened constantly, without freezing produce.

How often should you check?

The Food Code sets the limit but not a schedule. Your local health department, your HACCP plan or your corporate standard might. Common practice:

UnitTypical frequency
Walk-in cooler, reach-ins, freezersAt least twice a day: opening and closing
Prep rails / sandwich units opened all dayEvery 2–4 hours during service
Display casesOpening, mid-day and closing
Any unit after a repair or power cutHourly until stable

Twice a day is a minimum, not a goal. A unit that fails at 11 AM and is only checked at 10 PM can leave food out of temperature for 11 hours.

Where to put the thermometer

  • Every refrigeration unit needs a thermometer you can easily read, placed in the warmest part of the unit, usually near the door (§4-204.112).
  • Thermometers used for air must be accurate to ±3°F, and food probes to ±2°F (§4-203.11–.12). Check probes in ice water (32°F) every week or after they're dropped, and write the calibration on the log.
  • If the air reading is high, probe the food. A sealed container of soup in the back can be fine while the air near the door reads 44°F after a delivery.

What to write down

A useful fridge log has, for each reading:

  1. Date and time
  2. Temperature (°F or °C, but be consistent)
  3. Initials of the person who checked
  4. Corrective action, if out of range
  5. Re-check temperature and time
  6. Manager sign-off (weekly is fine)

When a reading is above 41°F

Writing "44°F" and walking away is the most common logging mistake. Inspectors look for what you did next:

  1. Probe the food. Is the food itself above 41°F, or only the air?
  2. Find the cause. Door left open, overloaded shelves blocking airflow, a delivery just loaded, a defrost cycle, a failing compressor.
  3. Protect the food. Move TCS food to another unit or into ice.
  4. Decide on discard. How long has the food been above 41°F? If you can't tell, that's a reason to check more often. Follow your health department's guidance.
  5. Re-check within 30 minutes and write it down.

Red flags inspectors notice

  • The same number every day ("38, 38, 38…"), all in the same pen: a sign it was filled in at once.
  • Readings logged for times when the business was closed.
  • Blank days with no explanation.
  • Out-of-range readings with no action.
  • A log that says 38°F while the unit's thermometer reads 47°F during the inspection.

Paper or app?

Paper works if someone owns it. It fails when the clipboard walks off, the person who checks is off sick, or nobody notices a blank day until the inspector does. A phone log can remind whoever's on shift, flag a high reading right away, and give you a PDF of the month. We compare both in paper vs digital temperature logs.

FAQ

How long should I keep fridge temperature logs?

The FDA Food Code doesn't set one retention period for routine logs, so it depends on your health department and your HACCP plan. Many operators keep at least the last several months on site and archive older ones. Ask your inspector.

Is a min/max or Bluetooth thermometer enough?

They're great for catching overnight failures, but you still need a record someone has reviewed. Write the min/max reading on the log each morning and reset it.

Do I need a separate log for each fridge?

One sheet per unit per month is easiest to read and file. A combined daily log works too, as long as every unit is listed clearly.
