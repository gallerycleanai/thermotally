Food cooling log: the 2-hour / 6-hour rule, explained

Updated 2026-10-25 · 7 min read · Based on the FDA Food Code

Improper cooling is one of the classic causes of foodborne illness outbreaks linked to restaurants, and one of the hardest rules to prove you followed without a log. Here's the rule, how to meet it, and how to record it.

The rule (FDA Food Code §3-501.14)

Cooked foodLimit
Stage 1From 135°F (57°C) to 70°F (21°C) within 2 hours
Stage 2From 70°F to 41°F (5°C) or below within the next 4 hours, so 6 hours total

Food made from room-temperature ingredients (for example tuna salad from canned tuna, or reconstituted foods) must reach 41°F within 4 hours (§3-501.14(B)). TCS food received at a higher legal temperature, like milk or shellstock at 45°F, must be cooled to 41°F within 4 hours (§3-501.14(C)).

Why stage 1 is shorter: bacteria such as Clostridium perfringens and Bacillus cereus grow fastest between roughly 125°F and 70°F. Getting through that band quickly matters most.

Methods that actually work

  • Shallow pans: food 2 inches deep or less, uncovered or loosely covered while cooling, on the top shelf of the walk-in with space for air to move (§3-501.15).
  • Ice-water bath: set the pot in a sink of ice and water and stir often.
  • Ice wands / cooling paddles: frozen paddles stirred through soups and sauces.
  • Add ice as an ingredient: make the base with less water, then add ice.
  • Divide large items: cut roasts into smaller pieces and portion big batches.
  • Blast chiller: the most reliable option, if you have one.

What doesn't work: a full 5-gallon stockpot straight into the walk-in. It often takes far longer than 6 hours to cool, and it warms everything around it.

What to record

  1. Date, food item and batch size
  2. Cooling method
  3. Start time and temperature (when it drops below 135°F)
  4. A 1-hour check (optional, but it tells you whether you'll make the 2-hour mark)
  5. 2-hour time and temperature: must be 70°F or below
  6. 4-hour check
  7. 6-hour time and temperature: must be 41°F or below
  8. Corrective action and initials

When you miss the limit

What happenedStandard corrective action (FDA guidance)
Not at 70°F by 2 hoursReheat to 165°F and start cooling again with a faster method, or discard
At 70°F in time, but not at 41°F by 6 hoursDiscard
You don't know when cooling startedTreat it as a failure; that's why the log exists

Your local health department may have its own procedure, so check its guidance or your HACCP plan.

FAQ

Can I cool food on the counter first?

Yes, as long as the clock starts at 135°F and you meet the 2-hour and 6-hour limits. Counter cooling is slow for large batches, so use an ice bath.

Do I need to log cooling for every batch?

To prove you met the rule, yes. Many health departments require cooling logs for every TCS batch, and some require them after a cooling violation. Check your local rules.

Does the 6 hours start at 135°F or at 70°F?

At 135°F. Total time from 135°F to 41°F is 6 hours maximum, and the first 2 of those hours must get the food to 70°F.
