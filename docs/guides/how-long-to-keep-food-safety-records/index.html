How long to keep food safety records in a restaurant

Updated 2026-10-25 · 6 min read · Based on the FDA Food Code

Short answer: the FDA Food Code sets a fixed retention period for only a few records: shellstock tags and raw fish parasite-destruction records (90 days) and certain reduced-oxygen packaging records (at least 6 months). It doesn't set one for routine temperature, cooling or cleaning logs, so your state or local health department and your HACCP plan decide. If nobody has told you, keeping routine logs for at least the period since your last two inspections, and ideally a year, is a sensible default. Ask your inspector to confirm.

Records with a fixed period in the FDA Food Code

RecordKeep forFDA Food Code
Shellstock (oyster, clam, mussel) tags or labels90 calendar days from the date the last shellfish from the container was sold or served (write that date on the tag)§3-203.12
Freezing records for raw or undercooked fish served ready-to-eat (parasite destruction), or the supplier's written statement90 calendar days beyond the time of service or sale§3-402.12
Cooling and cold-holding records for cook-chill or sous vide food packaged in-house under reduced-oxygen packaging without a varianceAt least 6 months§3-502.12
Sample report for a private (non-public) water systemThe most recent report on file§5-102.14

Your state may have adopted a different edition of the Food Code, with small differences. See which FDA Food Code your state uses.

Records the Code requires, without a fixed period

  • HACCP plan records, where a plan is required (for example for some specialized processes or variances): the plan itself and the monitoring, corrective-action and verification records it describes (§8-201.13–.14). The plan or your variance usually states how long to keep them.
  • Time as a public health control: written procedures kept on site and shown on request (§3-501.19).
  • Date marking system: explained to the inspector on request (§3-501.17(D)).

Routine logs: temperature, cooling, cleaning, sanitizer

For fridge and freezer logs, hot and cold holding, cooling, receiving, sanitizer tests, thermometer calibration and cleaning schedules, the FDA Food Code sets no national retention period. In practice:

  • Your health department may set one in its local code or in permit conditions. Some ask to see logs since the last inspection.
  • Your HACCP plan, if you have one, should state a period, and you must follow it.
  • Your insurer, landlord, franchisor or corporate standard may set its own.
  • If a customer complaint or illness report comes in, logs from that week are your evidence. Complaints can arrive weeks later.
A simple house policy: keep the current month at the unit, the last 3 months in a binder on site, and older months filed (or scanned) for at least 12 months. Write the policy on the binder cover so new managers follow it.

The FDA Food Traceability Rule (coming in 2028)

Separate from the Food Code, FDA's Food Traceability Rule (FSMA section 204) will require restaurants that buy foods on FDA's Food Traceability List (for example certain fresh produce, shell eggs, finfish, crustaceans, mollusks and soft cheeses) to keep receiving records with specific data, generally for 2 years (180 days for food bought directly from a farm). FDA has said it will not enforce the rule before July 20, 2028. Restaurants with average annual food sales of $250,000 or less (adjusted for inflation) are exempt, and in October 2026 FDA published draft guidance that, if finalized, would not enforce the rule for certain other smaller restaurants and retailers. Check FDA's Food Traceability Rule page for the current status.

Keeping a receiving log with supplier, product and lot or use-by information now is a good habit for either set of rules.

Paper or digital storage?

Most health departments accept either, as long as the records are complete, legible and available when asked. FDA's traceability rule explicitly allows paper, electronic records or true copies such as scans and photos. If you scan paper logs, keep the scans organized by month and unit so you can find a specific week quickly. More in paper vs digital temperature logs.

A records checklist for your binder

  1. Current permit and last inspection report
  2. Certified food protection manager certificate(s)
  3. Employee health policy and reporting agreement
  4. Temperature logs (by unit and month)
  5. Cooling, hot/cold holding, receiving, sanitizer and calibration logs
  6. TPHC written procedures, HACCP plan and variances, if any
  7. Shellstock tags (90 days) and fish freezing records or supplier letters (90 days)
  8. Pest control service reports
  9. Equipment service records (walk-in, dish machine, hood)

FAQ

How long do you keep temperature logs?

The FDA Food Code doesn't set a period for routine temperature logs. Follow your health department and HACCP plan; if neither sets one, keeping at least 12 months is a sensible default.

How long do you keep shellfish tags?

90 calendar days from the date the last shellstock from the container was sold or served, under FDA Food Code §3-203.12.

Can I throw away paper logs after scanning them?

Often yes, if the scans are complete and legible, but ask your health department first; some want originals for a period.
