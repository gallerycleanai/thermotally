Catering food transport and hot holding log: keeping food safe from kitchen to event

Updated 2026-10-25 · 7 min read · Based on the FDA Food Code

Short answer: catered food has to stay at the same temperatures as in a restaurant the whole way: hot food at 135°F or above and cold food at 41°F or below (FDA Food Code §3-501.16), unless you use time as a public health control with written procedures (§3-501.19). Probe and log each item when it leaves the kitchen, when it arrives, every 2 hours during service, and when service ends.

The temperatures that matter

StepLimitFDA Food Code
Hot food leaving the kitchen and during service135°F (57°C) or above§3-501.16(A)(1)
Cold food leaving the kitchen and during service41°F (5°C) or below§3-501.16(A)(2)
Cooked-and-chilled food reheated for hot holding165°F within 2 hours§3-403.11
Food cooked ahead and chilled135°F → 70°F in 2 h, → 41°F within 6 h§3-501.14
Time instead of temperatureUp to 4 h (or 6 h for cold food kept ≤70°F), marked and discarded at the limit§3-501.19

Before you load

  • Preheat hot carriers (fill with hot water for a while, or follow the maker's instructions) and pre-chill cold carriers. A cold insulated box takes heat out of hot food.
  • Get hot food hot. Cook or reheat to the required temperature, then pack it well above 135°F so you have a buffer for the trip.
  • Get cold food cold. Chill it to 41°F or below in the kitchen before it goes into a cooler with ice packs. Coolers keep food cold; they don't chill warm food.
  • Pack full. Insulated carriers hold temperature better when they're full; fill empty space with towels.
  • Probe and log every item as it's packed: food, temperature, time, initials.

On the road

Keep transport time as short as you can and keep hot and cold carriers apart. For long trips or hot days, consider powered hot boxes or a refrigerated vehicle. If the drive is long, check one item when you stop.

At the event

WhenWhat to do and log
On arrivalProbe each item. Hot food below 135°F that you know was hot when it left can be reheated to 165°F if you have equipment on site; otherwise follow your corrective action plan.
Setting up the buffetChafing dishes must hold food at 135°F; they're not designed to reheat. Light fuel or turn on heat before the food goes in. Cold items on ice need the ice up around the pan, not just under it.
Every 2 hours during serviceProbe each hot and cold item and write it down. Two hours leaves time to correct problems.
ReplenishingReplace pans instead of adding fresh food on top of old food.
End of serviceWrite what was discarded. Leftovers that were held correctly and are going back to the kitchen must be cooled using the 2-hour/6-hour rule; many caterers simply discard buffet leftovers.

When equipment can't keep up: TPHC

At venues without power or space for hot and cold holding, time as a public health control can be an option. Food must start at 135°F or above (or 41°F or below), be marked with a time 4 hours later, and be served or discarded by then. The 6-hour option is only for cold food that starts at 41°F or below and never goes above 70°F. You need written procedures, and some jurisdictions require approval or don't allow it for catering.

Corrective actions to write down

  • Hot food below 135°F: if you know it's been less than 2 hours, reheat to 165°F; if you don't know, discard it. (This 2-hour approach is common practice; follow your jurisdiction's rule.)
  • Cold food above 41°F: if it's been less than 2 hours, re-chill quickly; if you don't know, discard it.
  • TPHC food past its time: discard, and note the time.

Permits and paperwork

Catering rules vary: many jurisdictions require caterers to work from a licensed kitchen, and some require a separate permit for temporary events. Keep your log with the event paperwork. If a guest reports feeling ill, the log for that event is what shows the food was held safely.

FAQ

How long can catered food sit out?

If it's kept at 135°F or above, or 41°F or below, there's no Food Code time limit while it stays there. If it's held without temperature control under TPHC, the limit is 4 hours (or 6 hours for cold food kept at or below 70°F), with written procedures.

Can I reheat food in a chafing dish?

Chafing dishes are made to hold food, not reheat it. Reheat cooked and chilled food to 165°F within 2 hours using an oven or stove, then hold it at 135°F or above.

Do caterers need temperature logs?

It depends on your jurisdiction, permit and HACCP plan. Even where not required, a transport and holding log is your evidence if something goes wrong.
