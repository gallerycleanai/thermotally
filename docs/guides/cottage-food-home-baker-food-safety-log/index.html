Food safety logs for home and cottage food bakers

Updated 2026-10-25 · 7 min read · Based on the FDA Food Code

Short answer: cottage food laws are set by each state, not by the FDA Food Code, and most of them limit home bakers to foods that don't need refrigeration for safety, such as many breads, cookies and dry baked goods. Even when no inspector asks for logs, a simple record of fridge temperatures, cleaning, ingredients and batches protects you if a customer complains or your county asks questions, and it's the habit you'll need if you grow into a licensed kitchen.

First: what are you allowed to make?

Each state's cottage food law (and sometimes a county ordinance) decides which foods you can sell from a home kitchen, where you can sell them, whether you need a permit or training, and what goes on the label. Many states exclude foods that need time and temperature control for safety (TCS food in Food Code language), for example:

  • Cream, custard and cream cheese fillings or frostings
  • Cheesecakes and meringue pies
  • Foods with meat, fish or cooked vegetables
  • Cut fresh fruit

Rules vary a lot between states, and they change. Check your state's department of health or agriculture cottage food page before you add a product. If you want to make TCS foods, you usually need a licensed commercial or shared kitchen, where the full food code, inspections and logs apply. Our page on which FDA Food Code your state uses covers licensed kitchens.

A simple home bakery routine

  1. Before you start: no pets in the kitchen, hair tied back, hands washed, nobody sick handling food. Many cottage laws ban production by anyone with symptoms like vomiting or diarrhea.
  2. Clean, then sanitize counters, mixer bowls and tools. Unscented bleach solution or a food-contact sanitizer used at its label strength works; test strips tell you if it's right (see sanitizer ppm).
  3. Check the fridge and write the temperature.
  4. Bake and cool on clean racks, away from family food.
  5. Package and label with the information your state requires. Many states require a statement that the food was made in a home kitchen that isn't inspected, plus ingredients and allergens.
  6. Write the batch record: date, product, quantity, where it's going.

Allergens: the most common real-world problem

For small bakers, undeclared allergens are a bigger practical risk than temperature. The major food allergens under US law are milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame (sesame was added by the FASTER Act effective January 1, 2023). Keep a list of every ingredient in each product, check labels of the ingredients you buy (chocolate chips and sprinkles often contain milk or soy), and update your labels when you switch brands.

If you bake in a licensed or shared kitchen

Once you sell from a licensed kitchen (a commissary, a shared-use kitchen or your own storefront), the state food code applies in full. Expect to need temperature logs for coolers, cooling logs for anything cooked and chilled, sanitizer checks, a calibrated thermometer and date marking. FAQ

Do cottage food bakers need temperature logs?

Usually no state rule requires them, because most cottage food laws only allow foods that don't need refrigeration for safety. A fridge log for ingredients is still a cheap, useful habit. Check your state's cottage food rules.

Can I sell cheesecake or cream pies under cottage food law?

In many states, no, because they need refrigeration for safety. A few states allow more. Check your state's list of approved foods before you sell.

Will my home kitchen be inspected?

Many cottage food programs don't inspect home kitchens routinely, but some register or inspect, and most can investigate complaints. Rules differ by state and sometimes by county.
