What health inspectors look for in your temperature logs

Updated 2026-10-25 · 7 min read · Based on the FDA Food Code

Short answer: inspectors use your logs to answer one question: is this kitchen actually monitoring temperatures, and does it act when something is wrong? They look for complete entries (date, time, temperature, initials), believable numbers that match what they measure that day, corrective actions written next to every out-of-range reading, and a manager's review. A perfect-looking log with no variation can raise more questions than an honest one with a fixed problem.

Do inspectors have to look at logs?

The FDA Food Code doesn't require a general temperature log for every kitchen. It requires the temperatures themselves (41°F cold holding, 135°F hot holding, the cooling rule and so on), and it requires records in specific cases: HACCP plans (§8-201.14), time as a public health control procedures (§3-501.19), raw fish parasite destruction (§3-402.12) and shellstock tags (§3-203.12). Many state and local codes, HACCP plans and inspection programs go further and expect routine logs. And under the Code, the person in charge has to be able to show the inspector they know the rules (§2-102.11). Logs are the easiest way to do that.

What they check, line by line

CoolingStart time and temperature, 2-hour temperature (≤70°F) and final temperature (≤41°F within 6 hours)

Red flags that make inspectors dig deeper

  • Identical numbers: "38°F" twice a day for a month.
  • Same pen, same handwriting for a whole week across different shifts.
  • Entries in the future: the afternoon check already filled in at 10 AM.
  • Entries for closed days, or times when nobody was there.
  • Out-of-range readings with no action: "46°F" and nothing else.
  • A log that disagrees with reality: the sheet says 38°F, the unit reads 47°F and the food in it is at 46°F.
  • Cooling logs with only a final temperature. The 2-hour check is the part that matters most.
  • Logs kept in the office and filled in later, instead of on the unit or the line.
An honest log with an occasional 43°F reading and a clear corrective action ("door left open after delivery; food probed 40°F; re-check 39°F at 2:30 PM") shows a system that works. A spotless, identical log suggests nobody is really checking.

What a good corrective action entry looks like

Write what you found, what you did and the result:

  • "Reach-in 2 air 45°F, 11:05 AM. Probed chicken 43°F, moved to walk-in. Gasket torn, service called. Re-check 40°F at 11:40. JS"
  • "Chili cooling: 2 h reading 84°F. Reheated to 167°F at 4:10 PM, divided into 2-inch pans in ice bath. 70°F at 5:45 PM. MR"
  • "Hot well 128°F, unknown how long. Discarded rice (3 qt). Turned up well, re-check 150°F. AT"

Paper or digital?

Both are generally accepted if they're complete and available on request. Ask your health department if it has specific requirements for electronic records. What matters to an inspector is that entries are made at the time of the check, can't be quietly rewritten, and can be shown quickly. More in paper vs digital temperature logs.

A 5-minute weekly review

  1. Flip through every log for the week. Any blanks? Find out why.
  2. Any out-of-range readings? Check that each has an action and re-check.
  3. Any unit trending up (38, 39, 40, 41)? Call for service before it fails.
  4. Check that thermometers were calibrated (see how to calibrate a food thermometer).
  5. Initial and date the logs, and file them.

FAQ

Can I get a violation for incomplete temperature logs?

It depends on your jurisdiction and on whether logs are required for your operation (for example under a HACCP plan or TPHC procedures). Even where logs aren't required, gaps make it harder to show the inspector that temperatures are controlled.

Should I fill in a missed reading later?

No. Leave it blank and note why ("missed, short staffed"), then take a reading now. A back-filled number is worse than an honest gap.

How long do inspectors expect me to keep logs?

The FDA Food Code sets specific periods only for some records. See our guide on how long to keep food safety records, and ask your health department.
