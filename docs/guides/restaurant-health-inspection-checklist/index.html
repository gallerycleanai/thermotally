Restaurant health inspection checklist: a self-inspection based on the FDA Food Code

Updated 2026-10-25 · 8 min read · Based on the FDA Food Code

Short answer: inspectors focus on the five risk factors the FDA Food Code says are behind most foodborne illness from restaurants: improper holding temperatures, inadequate cooking, contaminated equipment, food from unsafe sources and poor personal hygiene. Walk your kitchen once a week with the checklist below, fix what you find, and keep your temperature, cooling and sanitizer logs where you can hand them over.

How inspections are scored

Most US health departments use inspection forms based on the FDA Food Code. The Code sorts its rules into three levels:

  • Priority items: rules that directly prevent illness, like cold holding at 41°F or cooking chicken to 165°F.
  • Priority foundation items: things that support them, like having a thermometer, a test kit, soap at the hand sink or written procedures.
  • Core items: general sanitation, maintenance and facilities.

Under the Code, priority and priority foundation violations should be corrected on the spot when possible, and otherwise within a short deadline: up to 72 hours for priority items and 10 calendar days for priority foundation items (§8-405.11). Your local scoring (letter grades, points, pass/fail) varies by jurisdiction.

Self-inspection checklist

1. Holding temperatures

  • Cold TCS food at 41°F or below in every unit, including prep rails and display cases (§3-501.16)
  • Hot-held food at 135°F or above (§3-501.16)
  • Each refrigeration unit has a working thermometer in its warmest spot (§4-204.112)
  • Temperature logs filled in, with corrective actions for out-of-range readings

2. Cooking, reheating and cooling

  • Probe thermometer on the line, accurate to ±2°F and recently checked in ice water (§4-203.11, §4-302.12)
  • Staff know the cooking temperatures for your menu: 165°F poultry, 155°F ground meat, 145°F whole-muscle meat, fish and eggs for immediate service (§3-401.11)
  • Reheated food for hot holding reaches 165°F within 2 hours (§3-403.11)
  • Nothing cooling in deep covered pots; cooling log shows the 2-hour and 6-hour checks (§3-501.14–.15)
  • Thawing only in the fridge, under cold running water, in the microwave right before cooking, or as part of cooking (§3-501.13)

3. Date marking and storage

  • Ready-to-eat TCS food kept more than 24 hours is labeled with a use-by day within 7 days (§3-501.17)
  • No expired or unlabeled containers
  • Raw meat stored below ready-to-eat food; food off the floor; containers covered
  • Chemicals stored away from and below food, all spray bottles labeled

4. Hands and employee health

  • Hand sinks accessible, not blocked or used for dishes, with soap, paper towels or a dryer, and warm water of at least 85°F (§5-202.12)
  • Staff wash hands when they should, and don't touch ready-to-eat food with bare hands (§3-301.11)
  • An employee health policy: staff know to report vomiting, diarrhea, jaundice and certain diagnosed illnesses to the person in charge (§2-201.11)
  • A person in charge on site who can answer the inspector's food safety questions, and a certified food protection manager where your code requires one (§2-102.11, §2-102.12)

5. Cleaning and sanitizing

  • Sanitizer buckets and the three-compartment sink at the label's ppm, with test strips on hand (§4-302.14, §4-501.114)
  • Wiping cloths kept in sanitizer between uses (§3-304.14)
  • Dish machine reaching its required temperature or sanitizer level
  • Food-contact surfaces in constant use cleaned at least every 4 hours (§4-602.11)
  • Slicers, can openers and ice machines clean

6. Food from approved sources

  • Invoices for deliveries; no home-prepared food in a licensed kitchen (§3-201.11)
  • Shellstock tags kept 90 days after the container is emptied (§3-203.12)
  • Deliveries checked and logged on arrival (see the receiving log)

7. Facility

  • No signs of pests: droppings, gnawing, live or dead insects; pest control records available
  • Hot and cold running water everywhere it's needed; no leaks
  • Floors, walls and ceilings clean and in good repair; lights shielded
  • Permit and most recent inspection report posted where your jurisdiction requires

During the inspection

  • Walk with the inspector and take notes; ask what each finding means and how to fix it.
  • Fix what you can on the spot (discard the warm food, remake the sanitizer, restock the hand sink). Corrected-on-site items are usually noted as such.
  • Ask which form or log format they prefer if they suggest changes.

FAQ

What do health inspectors check first?

It varies, but most go straight to the risk factors: hand sinks, cold and hot holding temperatures, cooling, cooking temperatures, sanitizer and the person in charge's knowledge.

How often are restaurants inspected?

It depends on your state or local health department and your risk category. Higher-risk menus are often inspected more often. Ask your health department.

Is this checklist the official one for my city?

No. It's a general self-inspection based on the FDA Food Code. Your health department's inspection form is the one that counts; many publish it online.
