Paper vs digital temperature logs: how long to keep them and when an app is worth it

Updated 2026-10-25 · 8 min read · Based on the FDA Food Code

Paper logs are free and every inspector understands them. So why do so many kitchens move to apps? Usually because paper fails quietly: blank days, lost binders and readings filled in after the fact. Here's an honest comparison.

Are digital logs accepted by health inspectors?

Generally, yes. Inspectors care that records are complete, accurate, dated, attributable (who took the reading) and available on request. The FDA Food Code doesn't require paper. Some jurisdictions do require a specific form or format, especially for HACCP plans and variances, so ask yours. A digital system should let you show records on screen and print or email them.

How long should you keep temperature logs?

There's no single national number for routine logs. It depends on:

  • Your health department. Many ask that recent logs be on site for review, often a few months.
  • Your HACCP plan or variance, if you have one. The approved plan sets the retention period.
  • Specific Food Code rules, for example shellstock tags kept for 90 days (§3-203.12) and parasite-destruction freezing records for sushi fish kept for 90 days (§3-402.12).

Practical approach: keep at least the last 90 days easily at hand, archive the rest for a year or more, and ask your inspector what they expect.

When an app is worth it

  • You've had a write-up for missing or incomplete logs.
  • More than two or three people take readings.
  • You cool food daily (cooling is the hardest log to keep on paper).
  • You run a truck or more than one site and the binder is never where the inspector is.
  • You spend time every week chasing staff to fill in sheets.

When it isn't: you're a one-person operation that already logs reliably, or your jurisdiction insists on its own paper form.

The 2026 FDA Food Code: written Food Safety Management Systems

The FDA released the 2026 Food Code in September 2026. The temperature limits on this site are unchanged, but FDA's summary of changes describes a new requirement (§8-201.15) for food establishments to have a written Food Safety Management System, with a phase-in period after a jurisdiction adopts the new code and an exemption for minimal-risk operations. It also adds a written employee illness policy and formal definitions for mobile food establishments and commissaries.

States adopt new editions on their own schedule, often years later, so nothing changes for you overnight. The direction is clear, though: more written procedures and more records. Read the FDA's own summary of changes and ask your health department when it plans to adopt it.

FAQ

Can I fill in logs at the end of the day?

You shouldn't. A log is supposed to record a reading at the time it was taken. Back-filled logs are easy to spot and undermine everything else you show an inspector.

Do I need wireless sensors?

Not to comply. A calibrated thermometer and a reliable log meet the rules. Sensors add 24/7 monitoring, which is valuable if a failed walk-in overnight would cost you a lot of stock.
