Date marking and the 7-day rule: how to label food in a restaurant

Updated 2026-10-25 · 7 min read · Based on the FDA Food Code

Short answer: ready-to-eat, time/temperature control for safety (TCS) food that you prepare and keep refrigerated for more than 24 hours must be marked with the day or date it has to be used or thrown out. At 41°F or below that's a maximum of 7 days, and the day you prepare it counts as day 1 (FDA Food Code §3-501.17). Food that's past its date, or has no date, must be discarded (§3-501.18).

Which foods need a date mark

All three must be true:

  1. Ready-to-eat (it won't be cooked again before serving)
  2. TCS food (it needs temperature control for safety: cooked meats, cooked rice and pasta, soups, sauces with dairy, cut melon, cut leafy greens, cut tomatoes, soft cheeses, deli meats and similar)
  3. Held refrigerated for more than 24 hours

Raw chicken that will be cooked doesn't need a 7-day date mark under this rule (though labeling it is still good practice). Tonight's tuna salad that will be gone by close tomorrow lunch, under 24 hours, doesn't either. Tuna salad that will be used across three days does.

How to count the 7 days

SituationDay 1 isUse or discard by
Food you prepared in-houseThe day you prepared itDay 7 (prepared Monday → discard by end of Sunday)
Commercially packaged RTE TCS food you openedThe day you opened the packageDay 7, or the manufacturer's use-by date if that's earlier and safety-based (§3-501.17(B))
A dish made by combining dated ingredientsKeeps the date of the earliest ingredientThat ingredient's discard date (§3-501.17(C))
Example: chicken roasted and chilled on Tuesday (day 1) is made into chicken salad on Thursday. The chicken salad keeps Tuesday as day 1, so it must be used or discarded by the end of the following Monday, not the following Wednesday.

Time in the freezer doesn't count

The 7-day clock pauses while food is frozen (§3-501.18(A)(1) excludes "time that the product is frozen"). If you make a batch of chili on Monday, hold it 2 days, then freeze it, it has 5 days left when it's thawed. Your label needs to show that: many kitchens write the prep date, the freeze date and the thaw date, or the remaining days.

Foods that are exempt

The Food Code exempts a few things from date marking:

  • Individual portions served or repackaged from a bulk container at a customer's request (§3-501.17(E))
  • Shellstock (live oysters, clams, mussels), which are tracked with tags instead (§3-501.17(F))
  • Some commercially made foods, once opened, from an inspected plant: certain deli salads made under federal manufacturing rules, hard cheeses and many semi-soft cheeses, cultured dairy like yogurt and sour cream, preserved fish, and shelf-stable dry fermented sausages and salt-cured products like pepperoni and prosciutto (§3-501.17(G))

Your state may use an older Food Code edition with a different exemption list. Check before you stop dating anything.

Date marking systems that work

The Food Code accepts any system that meets the rule, as long as you can explain it to the inspector (§3-501.17(D)). Common options:

  • Prep date + discard date on a label: "Made 3/4 · Use by 3/10". Easiest to read for new staff.
  • Discard day only, often with day-of-the-week labels: a "Sunday" label means "gone by end of Sunday".
  • Color-coded dots or tape, one color per day. Fast, but post the color key in the walk-in.

Whatever system you use, put the label where it can be seen without unstacking everything, and write the product name too. An inspector who finds an unlabeled container of a white sauce will assume the worst, and §3-501.18(A)(2) says food without a date must be discarded.

Make it part of a daily check

Date marking is checked by people, not thermometers. Add a line to your opening or closing checklist: "Walk-in and reach-ins checked for date labels; expired items discarded." Logging what you threw out also tells you which batches you're making too big.

Date marking also assumes the food stays at 41°F or below. If the walk-in runs warm, the 7-day assumption doesn't hold. That's one reason inspectors look at walk-in temperature logs and date labels together.

FAQ

What is the 7-day rule for food?

Under FDA Food Code §3-501.17, refrigerated ready-to-eat TCS food held more than 24 hours must be marked and used or discarded within 7 days at 41°F or below, counting the day of preparation (or opening) as day 1.

Is the day food is made day 0 or day 1?

Day 1. Food made on Monday must be used or discarded by the end of Sunday.

Does my state allow 7 days?

Most states base their rules on a version of the FDA Food Code, but a few use older editions or their own rules, and some local codes are stricter. Check your health department's adopted code.
