The Code also says food thermometers must be calibrated "in accordance with manufacturer's specifications as necessary to ensure their accuracy" (§4-502.11(B)). It doesn't set a schedule. Weekly, after a drop, and whenever a reading looks wrong is a common routine.

How to use the sheet

  1. Give every thermometer a short ID (P1, P2, "line probe", "walk-in dial") and write it on the thermometer with a marker or tape.
  2. Do an ice-point check: fill a glass with crushed ice, top up with cold water, stir, wait about 3 minutes, and hold the probe in the center without touching the glass. It should read 32°F.
  3. Write the reading, what it should read and how far off it is. If a Fahrenheit food probe is off by more than 2°F, adjust it (if it has a calibration nut or reset button) or take it out of service.
  4. Note the action and initial it. A manager signs off at the end of the month.

FAQ

Do I have to keep a thermometer calibration log?

The FDA Food Code requires accurate thermometers but doesn't require a calibration log by name. HACCP plans and many health departments expect a record of verification, and a log is the easiest way to show your temperature readings can be trusted.

Can I use the boiling-point method?

Yes, if you correct for altitude. Water boils at 212°F only at sea level, and lower the higher you are. Most kitchens find the ice-point method simpler and more reliable.

What about digital thermometers that can't be adjusted?

Check them the same way. If one reads outside the limit, replace it or send it back under warranty. Don't keep using it with a mental correction.
