Short answer: use the concentration on your sanitizer's EPA-registered label, and check it with a test kit every time you make a solution and regularly while it's in use (FDA Food Code §4-302.14, §4-501.116). For chlorine the Food Code links the ppm to water temperature and pH: 50–99 ppm needs water of at least 75°F (pH 8 or less) or 100°F (pH 10 or less). Quat is used at the label's ppm in water of at least 75°F. Write each reading down.

Quaternary ammonium (quat)

  • Concentration as stated on the manufacturer's label (and within the federal limits the Code refers to in §7-204.11)
  • Water at least 75°F (24°C)
  • Water hardness of 500 ppm or less, or whatever the label specifies

Iodine

  • 12.5–25 ppm
  • Water at least 68°F (20°C)
  • pH 5.0 or less, or no higher than the label allows

Any other sanitizer (peroxyacetic acid, acid-anionic, on-site generated products) must be used exactly as its EPA-registered label says (§4-501.114(E)–(F)).

"ppm" and "mg/L" mean the same thing for these solutions. Test strips are usually printed in ppm.

Contact time

Sanitizer only works if the surface stays wet long enough. The Food Code (§4-703.11(C)) sets minimum contact times, and the label may require longer:

SolutionMinimum contact time
Chlorine at 50 ppm with the temperature/pH combination above7 seconds
Other chlorine solutions in the chart10 seconds
Other chemical sanitizers (including quat)30 seconds

In practice, many quat labels ask for a minute or more on food-contact surfaces. Read yours and train staff to leave surfaces wet and let them air-dry.

How to test with strips

  1. Use the strip made for your chemical. Chlorine strips don't read quat, and quat strips don't read chlorine.
  2. Follow the strip's own instructions. Chlorine strips are usually dipped briefly; quat strips are usually held in the solution for several seconds.
  3. Compare the color right away in good light, and write the ppm on your log.
  4. Keep the vial closed and dry. Old or damp strips read wrong; check the expiry date.

Where to test, and how often

The Code requires a test kit but doesn't set a schedule. A routine that matches how solutions actually weaken:

SolutionWhen to test
Wiping-cloth bucketsWhen made, then every 2–4 hours. Food soil and cloths use up sanitizer, and §3-304.14 requires in-use cloths to be held in sanitizer at the proper concentration.
Three-compartment sink, sanitizing compartmentWhen filled and when the water looks dirty or cool
Chemical (low-temp) dish machineAt least daily, per the machine's instructions
Spray bottlesWhen filled; label each bottle with the chemical name

Common sanitizer mistakes inspectors write up

  • No test strips on site, or the wrong type of strip for the chemical in use.
  • Too weak: a bucket mixed in the morning and never changed.
  • Too strong: a "splash" of bleach well above the label range. More isn't safer; excess sanitizer can leave residue on food-contact surfaces.
  • Wrong water temperature for chlorine at lower concentrations, or quat in cold water.
  • Cloths on the counter between uses instead of in the bucket.
  • Bucket on the floor or next to food. Store chemical buckets off the floor and below or away from food (§3-304.14(E)).

Writing down corrective actions

When a reading is low, the action is simple: remake the solution, re-test, and write both readings: "Bucket 2, quat 100 ppm, remade, 300 ppm, 2:15 PM, JS". The re-test is what shows an inspector the problem was fixed.

FAQ

What ppm should quat sanitizer be?

Whatever your product's EPA-registered label says; quat products differ. Many are labeled for roughly 200–400 ppm on food-contact surfaces, but your label is the rule, and water should be at least 75°F.

What ppm should chlorine sanitizer be?

Under the Food Code chart, 50–99 ppm is common in kitchens, with water at least 75°F (pH 8 or less) or 100°F (pH 10 or less). Follow your bleach or chlorine product's label for food-contact surfaces.

Do I need to log sanitizer readings?

The Food Code requires a test kit, not a log. Many health departments and HACCP plans expect a record, and a log is your proof that you test.
