Short answer: a full stockpot will not cool fast enough in a walk-in on its own. Split the batch into shallow pans or smaller containers, stir it in an ice-water bath or with an ice wand, and leave it uncovered or loosely covered in the cooler. The target is the FDA Food Code cooling rule: 135°F to 70°F within 2 hours, and to 41°F within 6 hours total (§3-501.14). Log a temperature at the start, at 2 hours and at 6 hours.

Why big batches are the problem

Heat leaves food through its surface. A 5-gallon stockpot of soup has a lot of food and very little surface, so the center can stay warm for many hours, even in a walk-in. Thick foods (chili, refried beans, rice, mashed potatoes, gravy) are worse than thin broths because they don't move heat around by themselves. Bacteria such as Clostridium perfringens grow fastest in the middle temperatures, which is why the first stage (135°F to 70°F) has the tighter limit.

The Code also says cooling food must be arranged in the cooler for the best heat transfer, and be loosely covered or uncovered (if protected from overhead contamination) during cooling (§3-501.15(B)). Tight lids and stacked pans trap heat.

A routine for a 5-gallon pot of soup

  1. Turn off the heat and take the starting temperature as soon as it drops to 135°F. Write the time.
  2. Divide the soup into smaller containers or put the pot in an ice bath right away. Don't let it sit on the stove "to cool down a bit".
  3. Stir with an ice wand, or stir every 10–15 minutes in the ice bath.
  4. Check at 1 hour. If it's not near 100°F, add more ice or split it further now; you're at risk of missing the 2-hour mark.
  5. At 2 hours, it must be 70°F or below. Write the temperature and time.
  6. Move it into the walk-in in shallow, uncovered or loosely covered containers, on a top shelf with air around it.
  7. At or before 6 hours from the start, it must be 41°F or below. Write it, then cover, label and date it (see the 7-day date marking rule).

What if you miss the 2-hour mark?

If food hasn't reached 70°F within 2 hours, it didn't meet §3-501.14. Common practice, and what many health departments expect, is to reheat it to 165°F and start cooling again right away, or discard it. Write which one you did. If you miss the 6-hour mark for 41°F, discard it, and change the method for the next batch.

Food made from room-temperature ingredients

Tuna salad, reconstituted foods and similar items made from ingredients at room temperature must be cooled to 41°F within 4 hours (§3-501.14(B)). Pre-chilling ingredients makes this easy.

Cooling mistakes inspectors look for

  • Stockpots of soup or rice in the walk-in, covered with plastic wrap, still warm in the middle
  • Pans stacked directly on top of each other
  • No cooling log, or a log with only the final temperature (it should show the 2-hour check)
  • The same batch "cooled" overnight with no times recorded
  • Hot food in a reach-in that's now reading 50°F because it can't handle the heat load

FAQ

How long can soup cool before going in the fridge?

There's no separate "counter time". The whole cooling process must take soup from 135°F to 70°F within 2 hours and to 41°F within 6 hours. Start active cooling (ice bath, smaller containers) right away rather than leaving it on the stove.

Can I put hot soup directly in the walk-in?

You can, but a full pot won't cool fast enough and can warm the food around it. Split it into shallow or smaller containers and use an ice bath or ice wand first.

How often should I take temperatures while cooling?

The Food Code sets the limits, not the schedule. Checking at the start, at 1 hour, 2 hours, and then until it reaches 41°F lets you catch a slow batch in time.
