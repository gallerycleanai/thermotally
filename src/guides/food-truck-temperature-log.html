Short answer: a food truck follows the same temperature rules as a restaurant: cold food at 41°F or below, hot food at 135°F or above (FDA Food Code §3-501.16), and reheated food to 165°F within 2 hours (§3-403.11). What's different is the day. Log at the commissary when you load, every couple of hours during service, and at close, and keep the log in the truck where any inspector can see it.

Cooking temperatures (for example 165°F for poultry, 155°F for burgers made from ground beef, 145°F for whole-muscle steaks and fish) come from §3-401.11. Your county may use an older or newer edition of the Food Code with small differences; see which Food Code your state uses.

A service-day logging routine

WhenWhat to log
At the commissary, before loadingProbe food coming out of the commissary walk-in (should be 41°F or below). Note anything prepped and cooled the day before and its cooling log.
Truck units before you loadAir temperature in the truck fridge or cold rail. Pre-chill it; a warm rail plus a full load is the classic morning failure.
Hot food going into the wellReheat to 165°F and write the temperature. Don't use the steam well to reheat; it's built to hold, not heat.
Every 2 hours during serviceProbe each hot pan and each cold pan. Two hours leaves you time to fix a problem before food has been out of range for four.
After a power or generator interruptionProbe everything, write the time the power went out and came back.
ClosingFinal temperatures; what was discarded; what goes back to the commissary to be cooled, and its temperature.

Problems that are specific to trucks

The cold rail can't keep up

Prep rails on trucks are opened hundreds of times a service, often in a hot cab. If the top pans read 45°F, fix the setup rather than writing 41°F: fill pans less, keep backup pans in the reach-in below, swap pans more often, and keep the lid closed between orders. If that isn't enough, time as a public health control may be an option for some items, with written procedures.

Leftovers going back to the commissary

Hot food that comes back has to be cooled using the 2-hour/6-hour rule. Most trucks find it easier to discard hot leftovers than to cool them properly on a busy night. If you do cool them, log it on a cooling log.

Paper in a truck

Clipboards get wet, greasy and left behind at the commissary. Whatever you use, the log has to be in the truck when an inspector or event organizer asks. Some operators take a phone photo of each day's sheet as a backup.

More than one inspector

Trucks that work several cities or counties can answer to several health departments. Each may have its own form. A clean, consistent log that covers the basics (time, temperature, initials, action) is usually accepted, but ask each one.

FAQ

Does a food truck need a temperature log?

It depends on your health department and your menu. Many expect mobile units to show they monitor hot and cold holding, and a written log is the easiest proof. Ask the department that issues your permit.

How often should a food truck check food temperatures?

The FDA Food Code doesn't set a frequency. Every 2 hours during service is a common routine because it leaves time to correct a problem.

Can a food truck use time instead of temperature?

The Food Code allows time as a public health control (§3-501.19) with written procedures, a 4-hour limit (or 6 hours for cold food that stays at or below 70°F), and marked discard times. Some jurisdictions restrict or require approval, so check first.
