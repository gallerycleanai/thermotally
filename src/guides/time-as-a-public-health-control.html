Short answer: time as a public health control (TPHC) lets you hold food without keeping it hot or cold, for a limited time. Under FDA Food Code §3-501.19 the food must start at 41°F or below or 135°F or above, be marked with a discard time, and be served, cooked or thrown out within 4 hours. There's also a 6-hour option for cold food that never goes above 70°F. You need written procedures on site, and some health departments require approval.

Why kitchens use TPHC

  • Sushi rice, pizza toppings on a make line, sandwich stations or buffets where keeping every pan at 41°F isn't practical
  • Food trucks and events where hot or cold holding equipment is limited
  • Pre-portioned items set out at the start of a rush

The trade-off is strict: once food is on time control, the clock can't be paused or reset.

Written procedures: what they must cover

The Code requires written procedures, prepared in advance, kept in the establishment and shown to the inspector on request (§3-501.19(A)). They should cover how you meet each requirement above, and, for food that was cooked and cooled before it went on time, how you met the cooling rule. A one-page procedure per station is usually enough:

  1. Which foods are on TPHC, at which station
  2. 4-hour or 6-hour option
  3. How the starting temperature is checked and recorded
  4. How pans are marked (sticker, tape, color tag, written time)
  5. Who checks discard times and how often
  6. How food is discarded, and how that's recorded

What's not allowed

  • Putting food back under temperature control to stop the clock, or mixing a new batch into a pan that's already on time.
  • Unmarked pans. If there's no time on it, it must be discarded.
  • Raw eggs on TPHC in facilities serving a highly susceptible population, such as nursing homes or hospitals; those facilities also have other limits (§3-501.19(D)).
  • Using TPHC where your jurisdiction doesn't allow it, or without approval where approval is required. Many local codes have their own conditions.

How to log TPHC

The Food Code requires marking, not a log, but a log is how you show the system works and how you prove the starting temperature. Record one line per pan: food, station, starting temperature, time removed, 4 h or 6 h, discard time, whether it's labeled, temperature checks for the 6-hour option, and what happened at the end.

Common mistakes inspectors find

  • The time on the pan is when it was made, not when it left temperature control.
  • A 4-hour time written, but staff "top up" the pan with fresh food.
  • No written procedure on site, or one that doesn't match what the line actually does.
  • 6-hour option used in a hot kitchen with no temperature checks.
  • Food past its time still on the line at the end of a rush.

Is TPHC right for you?

If you can keep food at 41°F or 135°F with the equipment you have, that's usually simpler. TPHC is a good tool for stations where temperature control really doesn't work, as long as someone owns the clock. Timers, colored labels and a phone reminder at the discard time all help. FAQ

What is time as a public health control?

It's an FDA Food Code option (§3-501.19) that allows TCS food to be held without temperature control for up to 4 hours, or up to 6 hours for cold food kept at or below 70°F, with marking, written procedures and discard at the time limit.

Do I need approval from the health department to use TPHC?

The FDA Food Code requires written procedures available to the inspector. Many state and local codes also require you to submit or get them approved, so ask yours.

Can I put TPHC food back in the fridge?

No. The Food Code lists only three outcomes within the time limit: cook and serve, serve, or discard. Putting the food back under refrigeration to reset the clock isn't one of them.
