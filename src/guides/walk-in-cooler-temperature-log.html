Short answer: keep the food in your walk-in cooler at 41°F or below (FDA Food Code §3-501.16(A)(2)), read the walk-in's thermometer at least at opening and closing, write the time, temperature and your initials, and when it reads high, probe the food and write down what you did. Because a walk-in holds most of your inventory, it deserves more attention than any reach-in.

Why the walk-in is the log that matters most

A reach-in that fails costs you a few pans. A walk-in that fails overnight can cost a week of inventory and a day of service. It's also the unit an inspector is most likely to walk into, look at the thermometer, and compare with your log. The rules are the same as for any refrigerator. The difference is scale: more door openings, bigger deliveries, more places for warm air to hide.

Most operators set the walk-in to about 35–38°F. That leaves room for a busy delivery morning without freezing lettuce on the shelf nearest the evaporator.

Where the thermometer goes

The Food Code says a refrigeration unit's thermometer must be in the warmest part of the unit and easy to read (§4-204.112). In a walk-in that is usually near the door, not next to the evaporator fan, which is the coldest spot. Many walk-ins have a display outside the door. That's convenient, but its sensor may sit in a cold spot. Hang a second thermometer inside near the door and check that the two roughly agree.

  • Air reading: fast, good for the routine twice-a-day log.
  • Food reading: when the air is high, or once a day as a spot check, probe a container of something dense (a sauce, cooked rice, a case of raw chicken) in the front and the back. Food temperature is what the rule is about.

How often to check

The Food Code sets the limit, not the schedule. Your health department, HACCP plan or company standard may set one. A routine that works in most kitchens:

WhenWhat to record
OpeningAir temperature; min/max reading if you have one, then reset it
After the main delivery is put awayAir temperature, and probe one item from the delivery
Mid-shift on busy daysAir temperature
ClosingAir temperature; door closed and latched; curtain in place
After a repair or power cutEvery hour until it's stable below 41°F

A min/max thermometer is cheap insurance. It tells you the next morning whether the box went warm overnight, which a twice-a-day log can't.

What to write on a walk-in log

  1. Date and time of each reading
  2. Air temperature (and food temperature when you probe)
  3. Initials
  4. Corrective action, if above 41°F
  5. Re-check temperature and time
  6. Weekly manager review

When the walk-in reads above 41°F

Check the obvious things first, then decide about the food:

  1. Was it a defrost cycle or a delivery? Air temperatures can rise briefly during a defrost cycle or right after the door was propped open for a delivery. Probe the food. If the food is still at 41°F or below, write "air 44°F, defrost, food 39°F" and re-check in 30 minutes.
  2. Check the door. Gaskets torn, door not closing by itself, strip curtain missing, door propped with a box. These cause more warm walk-ins than failed compressors.
  3. Check airflow. Boxes stacked against the evaporator or up to the ceiling block cold air.
  4. Check the condenser and evaporator. Ice-packed evaporator coils, a dirty condenser or a fan that isn't spinning usually means a service call.
  5. Protect the food. If the food is above 41°F, move the most sensitive items (raw poultry, seafood, dairy, cooked food) to another unit or into ice.
  6. Decide on discard with your manager and your health department's guidance. How long the food was above 41°F matters, which is exactly why the log matters.
  7. Write it all down, including the re-check temperature and time.

Walk-in habits that keep the log boring

  • Don't put large pots of hot food straight into the walk-in to cool. Cool them first with shallow pans or an ice bath (see how to cool soup and large batches), or the whole box warms up.
  • Keep raw meat below ready-to-eat food, and keep a clear path to the thermometer.
  • Label and date ready-to-eat food that's kept more than 24 hours. That's the 7-day date marking rule.
  • Calibrate the probe you use for spot checks. A thermometer that reads 3°F low makes a 43°F walk-in look fine. Use the free calibration log.

FAQ

What temperature should a walk-in cooler be?

Food in it must be held at 41°F (5°C) or below under the FDA Food Code. Most kitchens set the unit to about 35–38°F so normal door openings don't push food over 41°F.

How often should I check walk-in cooler temperatures?

The FDA Food Code doesn't set a frequency. At least twice a day (opening and closing) is common practice, plus after deliveries and every hour after a repair. Check your health department's rules and your HACCP plan.

Is the digital display on the outside of the walk-in good enough?

It's useful, but its sensor may not be in the warmest spot. The Code wants the thermometer placed to read the warmest part of the unit, so keep a second thermometer near the door inside and probe food when in doubt.

Do I need a separate log for the walk-in freezer?

Yes, keep one sheet per unit. Freezers have different targets; see our freezer temperature log guide.
