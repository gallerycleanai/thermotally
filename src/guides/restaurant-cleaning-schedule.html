A cleaning schedule turns "keep it clean" into a list of jobs, how often, who does them, and proof they were done. It's usually the first written procedure inspectors ask about after temperatures.

What makes a good cleaning schedule

  • The task, specific enough to check ("slicer: disassemble, wash, rinse, sanitize, air-dry").
  • Frequency: every 4 hours, after use, daily, weekly, monthly.
  • Method and chemical, including the sanitizer and target concentration.
  • Who does it (role or name).
  • Initials when it's done, and a manager review.

Every 4 hours (or whenever you switch tasks)

The FDA Food Code requires food-contact surfaces and utensils used with TCS food to be cleaned at least every 4 hours when in continuous use (§4-602.11(C)), and also between raw animal food and ready-to-eat food, and whenever they may be contaminated.

  • Cutting boards, prep tables, knives and utensils
  • Sanitizer buckets for wiping cloths: change and test them (§4-302.14 requires a test kit)

Daily

  • Slicers, mixers, food processors after each use
  • Prep sinks, hand sinks (soap and towels stocked)
  • Fridge handles, gaskets and shelves wiped
  • Grill, flat-top, fryer exteriors; empty grease traps as needed
  • Floors, floor drains, mats
  • Trash and recycling emptied, bins cleaned
  • Dish machine: check wash and final rinse temperature or chemical sanitizer concentration
  • Restrooms

Weekly

  • Walk-in floor, walls and shelving
  • Hood filters
  • Ovens and fryer boil-out
  • Thermometer calibration in ice water

Monthly

  • Ice machine interior (or per the manufacturer's schedule)
  • Behind and under heavy equipment
  • Freezer defrost and deep clean
  • Ceiling vents and lights

Sanitizer basics

Follow the label. Common working ranges are about 50–100 ppm for chlorine and 200–400 ppm for quaternary ammonium, but the Food Code points you to the manufacturer's label and its own concentration and temperature table (§4-501.114). Test with strips at least each time you mix a new batch, and write the reading on the schedule.

Making it stick

  • Post it where the work happens, not in the office.
  • Assign tasks to shifts so "someone" becomes a specific person.
  • Have the manager review it weekly and initial it. Unsigned schedules suggest nobody checks.
  • Rotate weekly and monthly tasks onto specific days. "Monthly" with no date never happens.

FAQ

Is a cleaning schedule legally required?

The Food Code requires surfaces to be cleaned at set frequencies, but it doesn't mandate a written schedule in every case. Many health departments, and the UK's Safer Food, Better Business pack, expect one. It's also your best evidence.

Cleaning vs sanitizing: what's the difference?

Cleaning removes dirt and food residue with detergent. Sanitizing reduces germs to safe levels with heat or chemicals. Food-contact surfaces need both, in that order: wash, rinse, sanitize, air-dry.
